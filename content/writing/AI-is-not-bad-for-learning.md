AI can be good for your learning - really!

I’m getting tired of everyone blaming AI for their inability to learn, or understand their code, or are pearl clutching over its impending lobotomy of a generation as we all learn to let it do all our thinking for us.

Because none of that is necessarily true. We heard this a lot when Wikipedia was growing in prominence (back when I was in high school). We heard this about calculators (remember teachers telling us we won’t always have a calculator with us - that didn’t age well). 

The key thing these takes are omitting is the human agency component. AI is tool, a very powerful, very unclear how it is helpful in some cases, very cool, still rapidly evolving… tool. 

If you use it to do your thinking for you, it’ll happily do so. This isn’t new though, people have been copy pasting code they didn’t understand from websites since I was in college. People have copy pasted whole paragraphs from wikipedia and handing it in claiming it’s their work.

All these short cuts tend to prevent the user from learning and make it easy to jump straight to the answer. If your goal is to get to the answer as quickly as possible, this is excellent! IF, however, your goal is to learn something don’t use it this way. 

AI’s potential as a pedagogical super tool is unparalleled. As soon as chatGPT came out one of the first parallels from fiction that I have not been able to let go of was the primer from Snow Crash. A discarded smart book (AI) that helps a young girl out of poverty. 

While there are many concerns that are valid about how this affects the job market, one point I think is resolutely invalid is AI’s inability to help in learning. So long as you are aware it can help you learn AND you are willing to take time and struggle (this is the actual learning bit), it can help you do so much more efficiently.

Just to name a few examples:

- You can ask it to create a series of writing assignments for you, tell it what aspects of your writing you want to improve it, and watch it guide you along.
- You can ask it to create a curriculum of materials for you to follow, including testing questions. And then ask it to explain things you want to spend more time on.
- You can ask it to critique your thinking and reply in the socratic method to help you get better
- You can ask it to critique your system design proposal and highlight common pitfalls of your approach, alternatives to consider, etc.
- You can ask it to explain a line of reasoning, a piece of code, or the significance of a piece of news.

It may hallucinate at times under novel conditions, and if you try to trick it. But considering it is trained to effectively predict the next token and was trained on the entirety of human knowledge. It’s going to be a lot better than most tutors. 

Finally, the pitfall is absolutely there to use it purely to race to the finish line. But that’s the human impulse in us avoiding the uncomfortable feeling associated with learning and growth. You have to choose that route, because it’s harder. That has always been the case. This doesn’t mean you should always use it one way or the other, use it in whatever way is most helpful to you. Just don’t blame the tool for what is effectively a human choice.
